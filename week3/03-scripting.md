## The art of work (the hacker ethic)

I fully agree with this; Computers SHOULD make work easier for us. I also fundamentally believe that our work is what
makes us. At one point in my life; I would have considered myself a luddite-- minimal technology, minimal setup, minimal
everything. While I still partially believe in this form of digital minimalism; this overtime diminished gradually.

I still don't have-- nor would I like smart devices in my house to automate certain functionalities in my household.
I actually gave up an Alexa that I had won from a field trip to Google HQ from a competition back in Highschool because
I couldn't trust the device due to its proprietary nature. 

However, I still have several systems in-place on my devices that do automatic tasks; mostly menial tasks that I just
simply don't care or give a **hoot** about. 
>(hehe hoot, what a funny word.)

Well, that isn't to really diminish their importance. Most of those scripts automatically run to fix essential services like
my VPN connection to my home server should systemd stop the service because the system was suspended.

>(Now that I think about it, I should have setup [OpenRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenRC). Maybe if I had just gone with Gentoo instead of Arch...)

Alas there are some importance to work. I want to stray away from talking about AI because I am exhausted of talking about it--
but there was this great book that I read several years ago titled [_The Hacker Ethic_](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.5555/558235).
The argument that Himanen poses inside of this book was that there is a new type of work ethic that came from the 80's hacker culture
called the "hacker work ethic", where you find people who work hard and are driven by their work because they have such passion and love
for what they do. This was in contrast to the traditional protestant work ethic, where work was deemed to be suffering before enjoyment.

The thing about the hacker work ethic is that it doesn't necessarily have to apply to computer scientists-- it can apply to just about
anyone who loves the work that they do by pure passion. I don't really call myself a "hacker" to people because I find it to be cheesy 
granted that the work I do revolves around computer security-- but I would say that I do believe in the hacker work ethic and culture. 

## Free software
Although I was familiar with using linux since I was about 12, I didn't fully understand the importance of free software. 
Hell, I didn't even know what it was until I was 19 and up late at night reading about Richard Stallman along with the history 
of the GNU project and the GPL. That's when I started to understand the importance of free software and how much it really 
impacts my life and those around me. 

I started daily driving linux when I was about 19 because I was tired of not being able to control how my computer operated or worked.
A big reason why I didn't use Linux prior was because playing games was a nightmare with [WINE](https://www.winehq.org/), but Valve
came out with [protondb](https://www.protondb.com/). After switching, I hardly use my secondary windows install unless I want to play
a game that requires kernel level anti-cheat, or is just easier to run. A big reason why I don't use windows anymore is because my 
entire workflow is just better on Linux: I am far more productive; I have tools that I have written that make my work easier; and
I really love learning and knowing exactly how my system is running. Besides that, UNIX systems just feel better to program on and use
in comparison to NT systems-- my code feels much more clean.

## Modern Luddism
While I am profoundly pro-tech nowadays, I wasn't always so. In my teen years, I used to dislike technology. Sure I was probably the most inclined
to know much about it in highschool-- but truth be told, I think a large part of my anti-technology sentiments came from seeing how it was being
used to spy and trample on people's rights. Not to say that the system isn't doing that today (see [this note from week 0](../week0/00-writing.md)),
but there exists free alternatives to modern systems that respects the user. However, most people aren't keen on the fact that there are alternatives
and measures that allow them to prevent corporate/government surveillance-- therefore they choose the simple route to dislike technology as it comes.
I call this **modern luddism**. Like traditional luddism, modern luddism fundamentally believes that technological change is detrimental because 
of societal changes. However, the key difference between the two is that modern luddism still has technology woven into our lives.
