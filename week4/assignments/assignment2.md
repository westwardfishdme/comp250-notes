What do you think of when you think of a hacker's bedroom? Most would
think of dark rooms, cluttered with colorful text on multiple screens,
and circuit boards littered everywhere. As an amateur security
researcher myself this almost perfectly describes my bedroom, but how
would any average person know what hackers look like? For the most part,
we don't see the people who conduct these distant attacks from their
computers-- often miles or even countries away. However, we have this
idea of what a hacker looks like cemented into our brains: an anonymous
person wearing a hoodie-- obscuring their face, typing away at their
keyboard in a dark room. There is no surprise as to where this idea of
'what a hacker looks like' came from. The stereotype of the 'hacker' has
a deep and prolonged history stemming back to the late 1970s and early
1980s, and as a concept was cemented through film through their colorful
and creative depictions of hackers.

The term 'hacker' itself is ambiguous. A 'hacker' is often associated
with someone extremely talented in computer security, but even to this
day, those who work professionally with computers debate the meaning of
the term 'hacker'. Steven Levy, a journalist and editor for the popular
computer magazine Wired, wrote a book in 1984 called *Hackers: Heroes of
the Computer Revolution*. In his book, he describes 'hackers' as people
who are driven by the concepts of freedom, transparency, information
availability, and finding beauty and an appreciation for computer
technologies[@levy_hackers_1984]. Years later, Pekka Himanen, a Finnish philosopher wrote
an entire book describing the Hacker Ethic, where he describes the
'hacker work ethic' as someone who loves their work and is as passionate
as such[@himanen_hacker_2001]. This concept opens the idea of 'what a hacker is' to not only
programmers-- but carpenters, bakers, and all sorts of professions. So
how exactly did the term 'hacker' get associated with criminal behavior?
More importantly, is it even an appropriate term to be used to describe
cybercriminals?

For the sake of simplicity, the term "hacker" will describe anyone
related to the field of computer security, more specifically: the
stereotypical hacker. However, there are two ideas of hackers that are
necessary to keep in mind: the one defined by Levy and Himanen, and the
stereotypical one we created now. With that said, there are multiple
classifications of hackers regarding their ethical and legal standing,
defined through cybersecurity jargon. These terms often follow a lexical
pattern of color followed by 'hat' to discern whether a hacker is
breaking the law, or is hacking under legal premises. The main three
colors are White, Gray, and Black Hat hackers. A Black Hat hacker is
someone who hacks with total malicious intent on their targets, either
for money or for notoriety; Most cyber-criminals such as malware gangs,
ransom-ware gangs, or anyone looking for illicit financial or
informational gain fall under this category. In contrast, there is the
White Hat hacker. A White Hat hacker is someone who hacks with
permission and does so legally. In this category of hackers, you'll find
security researchers, ethical hackers, cybersecurity engineers, and
other professionals. 

Finally, there are the Gray Hat hackers who are the blend between the two. 
Gray Hat hackers are those who break the law, be it intentionally or unintentionally for any reason. 
These hackers are those who stand on the fine line of ethical and unethical hacking, and as the color describes, 
can have many different shades-- some more ethical than others, and the others the opposite. This color category is
often applied to hacktivists, non-malicious hackers who hack things that they are not legally allowed to, and hackers who break the law 
but do so in a morally righteous effort such as whistleblowers.

So with that said, how exactly did the film industry have an impact on
Hacker culture, and create the stereotypical hacker? This essay will
analyze two sources, the blockbuster film *Hackers (1995)* and the
award-winning show: *Mr.Robot* through lenses of accuracy, narrative,
and aesthetics about their influence on hackers across the globe. From
here, we can analyze the real-world impact films can have on
stereotypes, public perception, and their influence on their audience.

## Hackers (1995)

'Hackers' have always been around since computers could communicate with
one another. Due to the lack of effort placed into cybersecurity in the
early days of the internet, the early 1990s was a prime time to be a
'hacker'. The early 1990s proved to be a huge breakthrough for many
hackers. There were many influential moments in hacking history
experienced in the '90s: From the reign of the legendary hacker Kevin
Mitnick to the introduction of email and instant messaging-- the '90s
truly was a time where anything was possible for those with the right
mindset. The 1995 movie *Hackers (1995)* came out swinging with its
release in the smack-middle of all this commotion, aiming to portray a
story of a group of young teens demonstrating the immense power that
computers could have.

The film is notorious for its dated representation of 'what hacking
looks like', which is something that we will analyze in detail further
on. However, the film does capture some concepts accurately in regards
to cybersecurity. These concepts are core to understanding not only what
the field truly looks like-- but also highlight some low-level tactics
hackers will use in their endeavors.

The story starts with an opening shot of a quaint suburban house being
raided by federal agents, more specifically, a Special Weapons and
Tactics (SWAT) team. Now, SWAT teams are typically deployed for only a
few reasons-- some of those reasons include raids on international or
domestic terrorists within the United States, hostage situations,
barricaded suspects, or generally situations where it is too deadly and
dangerous for ordinary police officers to conduct a lawful arrest. SWAT
teams are often equipped with military equipment, and armored trucks,
and are highly trained in military close-quarter combat. They are also
infamously known for their behavior when conducting live raids-- often
aggressive by aiming their rifles at suspects' heads, as they are put on
the ground. In other words, these are the last people you would ever
want inside of your house outside of an invitation to the barbecue. The
scene finishes with our protagonist's room being raided by the officers,
and the screen fades to black.

As the screen fades back, we are introduced to a courtroom full of
people prosecuting an 11-year-old Dade Murphy, who is being charged with
crashing the US stock market through his hack of over a thousand Wall
Street computer systems. As the introduction concludes, we are then
presented with a step-print scene of a young Murphy entering a car
defeated post-trial. The film then fast-forwards to 7 years later, and
we are presented with overhead shots of New York City from a plane. New
York then transitions to a CGI graphic imitating that of a circuit
board, using the flow of electrons as cars, with the title of the film
overlaid.

The next scene follows Dade Murphy performing a social-engineering
attack on a TV station-- impersonating a desperate worker trying to
restore access to his work. He then successfully gains access to their
network by convincing the security worker to give him access. This is a
real tactic used by hackers, called "vishing" or phishing over voice.
Phishing is a method of gaining a victim's trust to get them to do
something a hacker wants-- be it to give up sensitive information or
for other nefarious purposes.

This scene litters itself with props we imagine in the hacker
stereotype: that dark room with the computer monitors and TVs flashing,
the lone perpetrator obscuring his identity through sunglasses, and
non-diegetic electronic music blaring in the background. The set itself
does not depict an accurate representation of hacking, but the picture
remains all the same-- Dade is performing a social engineering attack.

We are then introduced to Dade as a character. He just transferred to a
new school, and is picked on by his classmates, more specifically
Angelina Jolie's character Kate Libby, who tricks him into getting
locked out on the roof under the ruse of a 'pool on the roof'. We then
are presented to Dade hacking the school to get the same schedule as
her, and him meeting another hacker in his school. They later reconvene
at a roller rink arcade where Dade and his new friends meet up, and he
beats Kate's high score. After which, he goes home to hack the school's
fire prevention system, to get revenge for Kate's earlier stunt of
sending him to the roof. From this, we see that Dade is trying to fit
into his new environment, and find his calling and group, something that
we as teens-- often try to do.

In the first portion of the film, we see that Dade's friend group
depicts the early stages of Levy's hacker culture-- the constant
pursuit of knowledge, mixed with a deep appreciation for computer
technologies. As a hacker, there is no other rush greater than that of
finding that one big score-- the pure adrenaline rush of knowing that
somewhere, you have total control over something that you shouldn't have
control over. This rush is addicting-- the competitive sphere itself
intoxicating-- but with that, a consequence is just as equally
dangerous. Dade's friend Joey soon finds himself in a heap of trouble as
he accesses a Gibson computer belonging to an oil company. It is here,
that we are presented to our film's antagonist: Eugene Belford,
otherwise known under his hacker alias "The Plague". Belford is an
insider threat to this oil company, as he plans to rob them blind of
$25 million and capsize their coastal oil tankers-- causing millions
of dollars in damages and destroying the environment in the process.
Belford created a computer virus known as "Da Vinci" to enact this plan.
When Joey hacked into that Gibson computer, he accidentally copied a
file containing all of the information related to Belford's scheme.
Belford wants a clean escape with zero traces, and what other better way
than to frame Joey and his friends for his crimes?

Belford's character is devious-- deceptive not only to his employers
but to law enforcement as well. His actions are adaptive to the very
real threat insider threats pose to companies: a disgruntled employee
opting for the most unethical choice of all: betrayal. Belford is the
film's Black Hat hacker: he is the embodiment of what threatens
companies, and everyday people to this very day. Meanwhile, Dade and his
friends are more accurately described as Gray Hat hackers, as they are
'morally gray' when it comes to the nature of their hacks. Their hacks
don't involve theft, but they are not conducted legally. When they hack,
it is done for props between one another-- hacking for 'bragging
rights' and as a test of skill. However, when posed with a true threat
like Belford, we can see the true nature of our Gray Hat hackers shine,
and their moral ambiguity comes into question. The film perfectly
depicts this through their hacking montage of pranking and disrupting
the Secret Service agents tasked with taking them down with Belford.
Their actions are in protest of the arrest of their friend Joey, but
once they find out the truth of what Joey found, they begin to focus on
their main threat: "The Plague".

If hackers are known for anything, it is knowing their opponent better
than they know themselves. The concept of 'security through obscurity'
is a frowned-upon tactic in cybersecurity. To simplify what this term
means, and why it is something that is heavily frowned upon: 'security
through obscurity' relies solely on the belief that your adversary will
never be able to find a vulnerability in your system by changing something
rudimentary that doesn't actually perform any form of mitigation. 
While this tactic works on low-skilled hackers, and 'script-kiddies'
(people who copy and use scripts they find online), an even more
proficient hacker will eventually find this information regardless with
relative ease.

Belford's vulnerability was that he kept all of his dirty laundry in one
place. He firmly believed that he would be able to successfully stop
Dade and his friends from discovering his ultimate plan before they ever
had a chance to learn about it. What he had not accounted for was the
ingenuity, and raw talent of Dade's hacker group. Dade himself was able
to completely reverse engineer Belford's malware and was able to detail
what exactly Belford was doing to rob the company. Given the nature of
Dade's hacking skills, it was not only inevitable that they would
uncover this, but able to hand all of this information over to the
authorities, who were able to arrest Belford, and his accomplice--
highlighting the moment of their redemption. [@moreu_hackers_1995]

## Cultural Impact of Hackers (1995)
Earlier it was mentioned that *Hackers (1995)* was retroactively
notorious for its depiction of hacking. Whilst the flashy visuals and
usage of computer jargon are entertaining, it is not representative of
what hacking looks like. However, like the initial scene, there are
numerous scenes in the film where we get a glimpse of what 'hackers'
really do: Hackers will reverse engineer software, go dumpster diving,
or do anything to find the truth-- not out of spite, but from a love
for what they do. The film-- in this regard\-- almost perfectly
portrays Levy's definition of a hacker and is in line with Himanen's
Hacker Ethic. Our protagonist and company follow all of the necessary
guidelines to define a hacker: they believe in transparency and the free
flow of information, and despite all of the hardships and challenges
they face with, they are still extremely passionate in their pursuit.
Where the film lacks in its overly dramatized visuals and overuse of
complex jargon, the concept of 'hacker culture' flourishes.

This film captured many hearts of current-day hackers when it first came
out. In an article published by Vice in 2018, they asked many actual
hackers and cybersecurity specialists why they loved Hackers (1995) so
much, and their responses are pretty interesting[@davis_real_2018]. Most of them didn't
care that the usage of technology was inaccurate in the film, nor did
they care about its silly outrageous plot. What stuck with them mostly
is the aesthetics of the film and how it represented hackers and hacker
culture. One of my favorite responses from this article is as follows:

From March (alias) -- information security expert:

> "I like Hackers because, while being typically fantastical Hollywood
stuff, it is relatively grounded in what hackers could do: playing with
building automation systems to turn on sprinklers, hacking a mainframe,
etc. Of course, it's framed in psychedelic neon cyberpunk visuals, but
that's cool in itself. It also is one of the films that portray hackers
in a pretty good light---kids having fun, not being particularly evil,
with the villain being an evil corporate turncoat white hat. Not to
mention the soundtrack...bloody amazing" 

The movie's influence on hackers and for people wanting to become a
hacker is widespread. I remember from my childhood when I first watched
*Hackers (1995)* for the first time, I wanted to do exactly what they
did. I downloaded Kali Linux onto a USB and I would always carry it on
me wherever I went. I started studying network infrastructure, and how
the internet worked, and whenever asks me why I chose my career path I
could always point back to *Hackers (1995)* as the catalyst. However, as
I got older-- life happened and I wasn't so sure what I wanted to do as
a career anymore. It wasn't until I was in my late teen years when I
found *Mr. Robot* that my creativity, and inspiration for computers came
back.

Before we speak about *Mr. Robot*, there is some historical context that
needs to be addressed. If *Hackers (1995)* wanted to portray what
hacking looked like in the mid-1990s, *Mr. Robot* aimed to portray
hacking for the modern eye. Since the 1990's, a lot has changed
drastically-- not only technologically, but politically as well. Since
the conception of the internet, the world has become much more
interconnected. If you compare the internet today to that of the early
days of the internet, the early days were very similar to that of the
Wild West. With the realization of the potential that the internet had
for monetization, the internet arguably became much more centralized,
corporatized, and most importantly: scrutinized by government agencies.

9/11 was truly a turning point in our country's history. Not only did it
spark a war halfway across the planet-- one that affected millions of
lives, but it also led to two major events: the rise of hacktivism as a
means of civil disobedience, and an increase in government surveillance.
The early 2010s were crazy. Almost every other week there was a major
cyberattack being showcased on the news, and one name stuck out:
Anonymous. Anonymous is a decentralized hacktivist group that has no
official leader, and no official backing, and is a collective of many
different people from many religions, political beliefs, and
backgrounds.

To truly understand Anonymous, we have to look back to Las Vegas 2004.
Inside DEFCON 12, a convention for hackers to come together and share
exploits they've found or to give talks relating to computer security,
we find one of the most notorious hacktivists of all time, Jeremy
Hammond. Hammond was no stranger to controversy and law enforcement. At
this conference, Hammond spoke about the usage of hacking as a means of
civil disobedience[@hammond_electronic_2004]. After which, Hammond would be spoken to by a few
individuals from the FBI. Hammond would later join the ranks of
Anonymous and LulzSec, and execute one of the most notorious hacks of
our generation: the 2011 Christmas hack on RANE/Stratfor[@mccann_hacker_2020]. RANE/Stratfor
is a privatized intelligence agency working inside of the United States
using Open-Source Intelligence to its subscribers, primarily global
megacorporations. In 2011, Hammond leaked millions of emails from
Stratfor to Wikileaks, detailing the disturbing nature of their company.
Hammond's hack exposed the surveillance and tracking of the "Occupy Wall
Street movement", along with activists seeking justice for the 1984
Bhopal DOW Chemicals disaster. Hammond was arrested in his Bridgeport
apartment in Chicago, Illinois on March 5th, 2012, and was subsequently
sentenced to 10 years in prison.

Hammond's story is pivotal to the story of *Mr. Robot*. Not because the
story is inspired by him directly, but because the story of *Mr. Robot*
is inspired by his ideals of hacktivism, and it plays a part in what
hacking looks like in the 21st century. *Mr. Robot* comprises four
lengthy seasons following the story of our protagonist, Elliot Alderson,
played by Rami Malek. The entirety of the show is filmed through a
second-person narration, where Elliot directly speaks to the audience or
the "person inside of his head". The show was critically acclaimed after
the launch of its first season on the TV network USA and is famous for
its accurate depictions of hacking throughout the show-- often using
real-life technology as narrative devices in the show.

## Analyzing Mr. Robot

The first season outlines the story to be followed throughout the rest
of the series. In its introduction, Elliot hooks us with his soon-to-be
iconic "hello friend," and proceeds to deep dive into his conspiracy of
how the world is ruled by an elite cabal-- in his words, "the top 1% of
the 1%" and how they are after him. We then transition to a close-up
shot of a distressed Elliot riding the New York subway claiming that
they're tracking him from his actions the night before, generating a
narrative ellipsis to hook the audience deeper into his story.

We then transition to a lively cafe in the middle of the night. Elliot
walks up to the owner, Ron, and proceeds to have an awkward chat with
him. While the chat initially seemed friendly, Elliot proceeds to unveil
uncomfortable levels of information he gathered on Ron and additionally
praises him for his high-speed internet at his cafe. In his conversation
with Ron, Elliot details how the high-speed internet triggered
suspicion, more specifically, it "scratched that part of Elliot's
mind that wouldn't allow good to exist without condition". It is here
that the show begins to display Elliot's skill as a hacker, and
highlights the show's prowess in accuracy to the technology referenced
in the show. Here, Elliot reveals that Ron had been secretly running a
Dark Web service through Tor, hosting a child pornography website
through his Cafe's internet. Elliot details his exact actions--
sniffing through packets of internet traffic until he found a packet
that contained Tor's Onion Routing Protocol, and he performed his
investigations. This alone shows us that Elliot is not only extremely
talented but is also not afraid to break the law to do the right thing.
Elliot then proceeds to place a document on the table, claiming to have
total control over all of Ron's emails, and files, and with that--
total control of all of the incriminating data. All of which Elliot sent
anonymously over to the FBI for their investigation.

Shortly after his conversation with Ron, we learn that Elliot is
employed by AllSafe, with his friend Angela Moss. Elliot proceeds to
internally speak to the audience about how he hates his job, not because of
the people working for AllSafe-- but rather that the company is
protecting corporations. This is something that Elliot acclaims that he 
"couldn't hate more". We learn more about Elliot's hatred for the exploitation 
of capitalism in the following scene, where he speaks to his therapist. 
In response to his therapist asking him what about society disappoints him, 
he has the following internal dialogue:

> "Oh, I don't know. Is it that we collectively thought Steve Jobs was a
great man, even when we knew he made billions off the backs of children?
Or maybe it's that it feels like all our heroes are counterfeit, the
world itself just one big hoax. Spamming each other with our running
commentary of bullshit masquerading as insight, our social media faking
as intimacy. Or is it that we voted for this? Not with our rigged
elections-- but with our things, our property, our money. I\'m not
saying anything new. We all know why we do this, not because Hunger
Games books make us happy-- but because we wanna be sedated. Because
it's painful not to pretend because we're cowards. F*ck society."

From the first episode of the show, we learn 3 important things about
Elliot: Elliot suffers from mental health issues, Elliot despises our
hypercapitalist society, and Elliot is extremely gifted with computer
security. When the show's primary antagonist E-Corp is introduced in
the show, we are presented with a group of E-Corp employees walking into
AllSafe, and an advertisement for their company, along with Elliot's
thoughts on E-Corp. He tells the viewer that he calls them 'Evil Corp',
and not long after reveals that they were responsible for the death of
his father, and Angela's mother. After some time passes, Elliot gets
called into work at 4 AM to investigate a major cyberattack on E-Corp
servers. What initially seems to be a DDOS attack causing an outage is a
ploy for installing a rootkit on their servers, allowing the attacker to
gain total control over all processes on all computers that execute it.
When Elliot tries to uncover the source of the attack, he suspiciously
uncovers their identity with relative ease, to a group called fsociety.

In our introduction to 'fsociety' in the first episode, we meet Mr.
Robot, who attempts to convince Elliot to work with them to take down
E-Corp erasing all debt and causing a financial meltdown by targeting
E-Corp's data servers. While initially reluctant, Elliot joins them
after becoming irritated by the CTO of E-Corp's treatment of his friend
Angela. By the end of the first season, he pulls the trigger on E-Corp
wiping all of their servers and backups. In the process of deleting all
the debt, all digital records were destroyed, meaning no one can access
their property deeds, or bank statements, or perform any digital ACH
(debit, or credit card) transactions. This makes people lose all trust
in E-Corp, and many disgruntled individuals join 'fsociety' because of
this.

What is interesting about 'fsociety' is that they sprout up seemingly
out of nowhere but are extremely influential, much akin to its real-life
counterpart Anonymous. The show at points makes it abundantly clear that
'fsociety' is the show's rebrand of the Anonymous group. Both Anonymous
and 'fsociety' are decentralized, anti-corporate technological
anarchists that wear masks and use hacking as a means of protesting and
civil disobedience. In the second season, we are even presented with an
analogous parallel to Jeremy Hammond's story where fsociety uncovers
government surveillance on groups connected with fsociety and has begun
illegally spying on US citizens [@satter_wikileaks_2012]. 

Much like *Hackers (1995)*, Mr.Robot had been just as influential as the
movie from the '90s had been, albeit with much more impact than prior.
like the show, Baptiste has a legitimate side; He runs a company that
reverse engineers apps on Android devices and looks for security
vulnerabilities, however, when there is something that is more legally
gray, he puts on a Gray Hat and becomes Elliot Alderson.

Robert Baptiste is an ethical hacker from France. In June 2015, he
created an account on Twitter under the handle fs0c131y. As of April
2024, his account has over 245k followers and is still active. He did an
interview in 2022 with Jack Rhysider[@rhysider_this_2022], 
the host of the Darknet Diaries podcast which interviews hackers and discusses global 
cyberattacks, and cybercrime. In this interview, he goes by either the alias Elliot 
or by his actual name Robert. Initially, when Rhysider came across his
account, he thought the account was just a fan page or a gimmick for the
show, but it turns out 'Elliot' posts actual information about security
vulnerabilities he had found or information on reverse engineering. Much
like the show, Baptiste has a legitimate side; He runs a company that
reverse engineers apps on Android devices and looks for security
vulnerabilities, however, when there is something that is more legally
gray, he puts on a Gray Hat and becomes Elliot Alderson.

While the real Elliot Alderson is much more ethical than the show, the
real-life Evil Corp is far more sinister. In 2022, BBC did an
investigative report into a Russian Hacker group called "Evil Corp", and
attempted to interview their leader Maksim Yakubets[@goudsmit_russian_2021]. Yakubets and his
collective had been responsible for about 100 million dollars in damages
due to cyberattacks, and Yakubets himself is listed as one of the FBI's
most wanted. As of 2024, Yakubets is living lavishly rich in the Russian
Federation, and will not be extradited to the United States anytime
soon[@national_crime_agency_evil_2024].
If there is anything that hackers love other than computers, it is video
games. Video games are an awesome medium to wind down, enjoy, and just
relax. In 2016, Hello Games launched one of their most ambitious
projects yet: No Man's Sky. No Man's Sky is a space exploration game
that allows players to connect to game servers and explore an entire
universe. The game was to feature fully functional multiplayer, massive
worlds to explore, and endless planets to explore. During the launch of
the game, many features that had been advertised were either missing
from the game or completely lacklustered [@kain_no_2016]. This caused outrage from many
fans who were looking forward to the game's launch. Shortly after, a
group of disgruntled hackers were able to hack into Hello Game's chief
Sean Murray's Twitter account, and posted the following tweet:

"Server hacked. We're binging Mr Robot Episodes as quickly as we can
looking for answers. Ep05 is a cracker."

Shortly after this tweet went live, the moderators of the official
subreddit for No Man's Sky briefly changed the entire subreddit to one
dedicated to *Mr. Robot*[@kain_no_2016-1]. After a couple of days, the subreddit reverted
to being dedicated to the game, and Sean Murray gained access to his
account again.

## Conclusion

So where does the line between fiction and non-fiction begin? Both the
show and movie *Mr. Robot* and Hackers (1996) are just that: films and
shows for our entertainment. We watch these shows and movies as
outsiders to the perspectives of the worlds that films create and build
for us. We experience their world from our perspectives, our
preconceptions of what the world is, and what their world presents. We
create analogous parallels from their world into ours and we create
these stereotypes. The word stereotype as defined by Merriam-Webster is
as follows:

> Stereotype (noun): a standardized mental picture that is
held in common by members of a group and that represents an
oversimplified opinion, prejudiced attitude, or uncritical judgment.

Films are the perfect form of media to create stereotypes. Life is not a
linear story, and can't be described as such. Directors and producers
can pour as many hours, and days into researching the correct way
something should be done, but at the end of the day, they must simplify

accurate representation of hacking that I've seen myself and that many
other hackers have seen, but it still can't compare to the real-life
thing, yet we see that it encapsulates the hearts of many hackers, and
non-hackers alike.
